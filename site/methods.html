Methods

Not an official product. Beach Thermometer is an experimental overview from Tini Scientific. It brings together water temperature and water level measurements from many monitoring networks so that resource managers, public service staff and the public can see, in one place, where ocean conditions along the coasts of Washington, Oregon and California are changing. Its flags are automated and use their own methods, so they won't always match official products. For official information, see the official sources listed below.

Where the data comes from, and when

The site updates every hour, at about 20 minutes past the hour (Pacific time), when it collects the latest readings from each network. Most stations report hourly or more often; networks usually publish readings 25–50 minutes after they're taken, so the newest value on the map is typically under two hours old. Each station's card shows the time of its latest reading.

Readings are averaged into hourly values. The site keeps each station's past 45 days for its chart; each station's chart data can be downloaded as a CSV file from its card. The chart's 90 days and Year views show daily means (UTC days with at least 12 hourly values, or half the usual readings for a station that reports less often: the Hood Canal profiling buoys report every 6 hours), the Year view for the past 365 days. Days before September 2026, when the site's own record begins, were filled once from the operators' archives: NDBC's yearly and monthly files for buoys, the CO-OPS data API (without flagged readings) for tide stations, and the ERDDAP servers for moorings and shore stations. Days without water temperature in those archives are left as gaps. Real-time data from all of these networks is preliminary: operators may later correct or remove readings.

Water temperature: checks and processing

  • Buoys (NDBC): missing-value codes are removed; the readings are otherwise used as published.
  • Tide stations (CO-OPS): 6-minute readings that NOAA's automated checks flag (a sudden jump, or a value beyond the sensor's limits) are dropped, both in the recent data and in the historical record used for the normal range. NDBC's copy of these readings carries no flags, so the site reads them from CO-OPS directly.
  • Moorings and shore stations (IOOS, NANOOS): at moorings, only near-surface sensors are used (the shallowest within 3 m), to match the buoys. Shore stations report every sensor at the surface (0 m), so this check can't tell how deep they are; see the station table for what is known. Readings that failed the operator's automated quality checks are dropped, as are values outside −2 to 40 °C (some records contain fill values) and spikes more than 2 °C away from the median of the surrounding 6 hours.
  • Profiling buoys in Puget Sound and Hood Canal (University of Washington's Twanoh, Hoodsport, Hansville, Point Wells and Carr Inlet buoys): a sensor travels from the bottom to near the surface every few hours rather than measuring continuously. Each profile's shallowest good reading within 3 m of the surface is used (usually 1.5–3 m), so these stations have a few readings a day instead of hourly ones.
  • Stations that stop reporting: a station whose latest reading is more than 14 days old is shown as a hollow marker with "no water temperature in the past 14 days", and is left out of the heatwave counts.

Normal ranges (climatology)

Each station is compared with its own history: what is normal at a harbor differs from what is normal 20 miles offshore. For every station with a long enough record, the site estimates, for each day of the year, the normal (average) water temperature and the normal range, from the 10th to the 90th percentile. The method follows the marine heatwave definition of Hobday et al. (2016):

  1. Daily means are computed from hourly values, counting only days with at least 12 hours of data.
  2. Reference period: the World Meteorological Organization's standard 1991–2020 period (WMO 2017), when the station has at least 15 years of data in it. Otherwise all of the station's complete years are used, if there are at least 8. A year counts if it has at least 60 days of data. Stations with shorter records show temperatures without a normal range. Each station's card lists the years its normal range is based on.
  3. Tide stations: normal ranges use data from 2005 on. NOAA's temperature records at many tide stations go back to the 1990s, but the earlier years include multi-year stretches that are several degrees off and not flagged. One exception to the reference period: Tacoma uses 2015–2025. Its water ran 1–1.5 °C (about 2–3 °F) colder in 2008–2012 than at any other time in its record, and colder than any other gauge on the coast in those years, and it has stayed about 0.6 °C (1 °F) warmer than its 2005–2020 average every year since 2015. A 2005–2020 normal would flag ordinary recent temperatures there as suspected marine heatwaves.
  4. Years left out: each station's monthly departures from its normal were compared with those of its neighbors (within 150 km) to find stretches of wrong readings that the operators' automated checks missed. Six stations have years left out of their normal range: Port Angeles 2006–2007 (about 1 °C below New Dungeness and Port Townsend, which it otherwise matches within 0.2 °C), Toke Point 2006–2007 (about 2.5 °C above Westport and the Grays Harbor buoy, against a usual 0.5–1 °C), Crescent City Harbor 2005 (readings climbing to 21–41 °C), Richmond 2007 (21–25 °C in winter and spring), Point Reyes (Drakes Bay) 2005–2006 (about 3 °C below its neighbors) and Point Loma South 2006–2007 (1–3 °C below the Torrey Pines buoy nearby). Shorter or smaller departures of about 1 °C, which can be real at harbors and bays, were kept.
  5. Day-of-year statistics: for each day of the year, all daily means within 5 days either side (an 11-day window) across the reference years are pooled, and their mean and 10th and 90th percentiles are taken. At least 40 daily means are required.
  6. Smoothing: each statistic is smoothed with a 31-day running mean that wraps around the end of the year, so that short records don't give a jagged seasonal cycle.

Historical data comes from NDBC's yearly archives for buoys (NDBC's archive of CDIP buoys begins in 2004), from the CO-OPS data API's hourly readings for tide stations (without flagged readings), and from the IOOS sensor catalog, NANOOS and the University of Washington for moorings and shore stations. Records of different lengths mean that normal ranges at different stations cover different years; stations whose normal is based on recent years only will partly include recent warming in their "normal".

Suspected marine heatwaves and other labels

Each station with a normal range gets one label, checked in this order:

Station labels
LabelRule
Suspected marine heatwaveThe daily mean has been above the station's 90th percentile for the date for at least 5 days in a row, up to the latest day.
Suspected marine cold spellThe daily mean has been below the 10th percentile for at least 5 days in a row, up to the latest day.
Warmer or cooler than usualThe average of the past 24 hours is more than 0.25 °C (0.45 °F) above or below the normal for the date.
Near normalWithin 0.25 °C of the normal.
No status · readings in questionThe station's recent readings look wrong: for example, a lasting jump that nearby stations don't show. Its temperature and chart are still shown, with a note on its card, but it gets no other label and isn't counted. As of November 2026 this applies to Port Townsend, whose readings rose about 2.5 °C (4.5 °F) on June 7, 2026 and have stayed that much higher. Its daily ups and downs didn't change, so the sensor doesn't appear to have moved closer to the surface.

The 90th-percentile, 5-day rule is the core of Hobday et al.'s (2016) marine heatwave definition, and the cold spell rule is its mirror image (Schlegel et al. 2021). The streak shown in a card ("Suspected marine heatwave · 12 days") is the number of days in a row so far. A streak as long as the 45 days the site keeps is shown as "45+", since it may have started earlier.

The flags are called suspected because they are simpler than a full marine heatwave analysis:

  • They are based on preliminary, real-time measurements at single points, not on reviewed data or satellite maps of the wider ocean.
  • A heatwave must run up to the latest day; Hobday et al. also join events separated by gaps of 2 days or less, which this site does not.
  • Reference periods differ between stations, and some are shorter than the 30 years Hobday et al. recommend.
  • Heatwaves are not graded by intensity (the moderate to extreme categories of Hobday et al. 2018).

Tides and flooding

The Tides & flooding view shows the 24 NOAA tide stations, using water level data from the CO-OPS data API, collected every hour.

  • Measured water level: 6-minute readings, in feet or meters above Mean Lower Low Water (MLLW, the average of each day's lowest tide), the same scale as tide tables. Each card gives how far the water is now from the nearest flood level, and NOAA's next predicted high tide, as published in its tide tables. Recent readings are preliminary: NOAA verifies them weeks to months later and may revise them. Each station's card offers its past 30 days of 6-minute water levels, predicted tides and their difference as a CSV file. Readings that NOAA's automated checks flag for a sudden jump or a value beyond the sensor's limits are dropped. Charts use readings every 30 minutes over the past 30 days.
  • Predicted tide: NOAA's tide predictions for each station, calculated from its tidal constituents (harmonic analysis of its long record), for the past 30 days and the next 48 hours.
  • Water level vs. predicted tide: the measured level minus the predicted tide, averaged over the past hour. It shows how much higher or lower the water is than the tide alone would make it, from causes such as storms (wind and low air pressure), river flow, and slower ocean changes such as El Niño and warm water. This difference is also called the non-tidal residual (Pugh & Woodworth 2014). Markers are colored by it, on a fixed scale of ±3 ft.
  • 25-hour average: the chart of measured minus predicted also shows its trailing 25-hour average, which smooths out what remains of the tide in the difference (the simplest form of the tidal filter described by Godin 1972; see also Thomson & Emery 2014). Because it averages the previous 25 hours, it lags real changes by about 12 hours.

Flood levels

Flood levels are the National Weather Service's minor, moderate and major coastal flood thresholds for each station, as published in NOAA's station metadata. These are the water levels at which flooding begins to affect the area near the gauge. As of November 2026, 8 of the 15 stations have a minor flood level, 3 of those also have a moderate level, and none has a major level; stations without NWS levels show water levels but no flood status.

A station is marked as having reached a flood level when the measured water level stayed at or above that level for at least two readings in a row (12 minutes) during the past hour, so a single unusual reading can't raise it. Markers show one, two or three rings for minor, moderate or major. A station whose latest reading is more than 3 hours old gets no flood status.

A flood level reached at a gauge does not mean that flooding is happening everywhere nearby, and flooding can happen while a gauge stays below its flood level: gauges measure the still water level in harbors, not waves running up beaches, and local flooding depends on the shape of the shore. These flags are not warnings; the National Weather Service issues official coastal flood advisories and warnings.

The daily email

Once a day, at about 7:20 am Pacific time, the site posts a summary to its feed (and the daily email built from it): which stations started or ended a suspected marine heatwave since the day before, and which are continuing, grouped by region from north to south. A station that stops reporting during a heatwave is listed separately and not counted as ended. The summary uses the same rules as the map.

Limitations

  • Measurements are from fixed points. Conditions between stations, offshore and below the surface can differ.
  • Real-time data is preliminary and can contain errors that the automated checks miss; operators may correct it later.
  • Stations differ in sensor depth, setting (open ocean, bay, harbor, pier, seawater intake) and record length, which affects how comparable they are. The buoys and moorings measure within 3 m of the surface; tide-station and intake sensors can be several meters deeper, where the water is often cooler and changes less from day to day. Each station is compared only with its own normal, so its labels are fair for that spot, but its temperature is not always the temperature at the surface.
  • Normal ranges for stations with short records are less certain, and may partly reflect recent warming.
  • The site depends on each network's servers. When a source is unavailable, its stations show no current reading until it returns, and the Tides & flooding view is turned off if NOAA's water level service can't be reached.

Getting official flood alerts

The National Weather Service no longer sends alerts by email, but its coastal flood watches, warnings and advisories can be received these ways:

  • Your county's emergency alerts. Each county runs its own alert system (by text, phone call or email), which can include coastal flooding. Each state lists the sign-up pages: CalAlerts.org for California (recommended by the California Governor's Office of Emergency Services), OR-Alert for Oregon, and the Washington Emergency Management Division's alerts page for Washington.
  • The FEMA app (free, for iPhone and Android) sends National Weather Service watches, warnings and advisories for up to five locations you choose. FEMA mobile products.
  • The National Weather Service's alert feed for coastal flooding in Washington, Oregon and California, which a feed reader (or a feed-to-email service) can follow: NWS West Coast coastal flood alerts (Atom feed).
  • NOAA Weather Radio, which broadcasts all National Weather Service alerts for your area.

Wireless Emergency Alerts, which reach phones without signing up, are used for the most dangerous warnings and usually not for coastal flood advisories.
